Early Years

Merle Haggard was born 6, 1937, in Oildale, California, just outside Bakersfield, to Okie parents who'd fled the Dust Bowl and were living in a converted boxcar. His father, James, died when Merle was nine, a loss that seems to have knocked the boy off course — he began skipping school, running away, and getting picked up for truancy and petty theft. Through his teens he cycled in and out of juvenile institutions, including reform school at Preston, for offenses ranging from shoplifting to car theft. He kept slipping back into trouble even after brief stretches of going straight, and in 1957 a botched burglary attempt landed him in San Quentin. It was there, as legend and Haggard himself told it, that he saw Johnny Cash play a New Year's Day show for the inmates in 1958 — a moment that pointed him toward music as something more than a pastime. Paroled in 1960, he returned to Bakersfield and started working the local honky-tonk circuit, playing guitar and bass in bands around town, including time backing Wynn Stewart. He signed with the small Tally Records label and released his first single, "Skid Row," in 1963, followed by other early sides. By 1964 he was gaining traction as a singer in his own right — cutting records like "(My Friend) The Wine Died" and dueting with Bonnie Owens — right on the edge of the breakthrough that would come with "Sing Me Back Home" and the string of hits later in the decade.

Merle Haggard: 1964-1973

By 1965, Haggard had moved to Capitol Records and formed his backing band, Strangers. That year "(I'm A) Lonesome Fugitive" became his first No. 1 hit, and its ex-con narrative — not far from his own past — set the tone for the persona he'd build his career on. Hits kept coming through the mid-60s: "Swinging Doors," "The Fugitive," "Branded Man," "Sing Me Back Home," and "Mama Tried" (1968), each one leaning into themes of prison, poverty, and hard living drawn straight from his own biography. In 1967 he released "I'm a Lonesome Fugitive" and by decade's end had become one of the defining voices of the "Bakersfield sound," a grittier, harder-edged answer to the polished Nashville country coming out of the same era. His songwriting increasingly doubled as autobiography and social commentary. Then came 1969's "Okie from Muskogee," a song that turned him into a national figure almost overnight — embraced as an anthem of working-class, anti-counterculture pride, and just as quickly read by others as a conservative broadside against hippies and campus protest. Whatever the intent, it made Haggard a household name and a lightning rod, and the follow-up "Fightin' Side of Me" doubled down on the same territory. The early 1970s were arguably his commercial and critical peak. He kept racking up No. 1 country singles — "We'll Look Back," "Grandma Harp," "Carolyn," "It's Not Love (But It's Not Bad)" — and in 1973 received a pardon from California governor Ronald Reagan for his earlier crimes, formally closing the book on the San Quentin chapter. He was named the Country Music Association's Entertainer of the Year in 1970, and through 1973 he continued to tour heavily and record prolifically, cementing his stature as one of country music's most important and most complicated stars.

Merle Haggard: 1973-1985

Haggard's momentum carried straight through the mid-70s, with hits like "We Make It Through December" (1973), "Man from the Mountain," and "Movin' On" (the theme song for the NBC trucking drama of the same name). In 1977 he released his tribute to Bob Wills, "A Tribute to the Best Damn Fiddle Player in the World," reconnecting with the Western swing sounds he'd grown up on and bringing some of Bob Wills's old Texas Playboys into the studio with him. By the late 1970s he'd left Capitol for MCA, then moved to Epic Records in 1981, where producer Billy Sherrill helped spark a second commercial peak. 1981's "Rainbow Stew" showed him folding a harder-edged, more contemporary sound into his catalog, and in 1982 "Are the Good Times Really Over (I Wish a Buck Was Still Silver)" became another defining single, a wistful, half-satirical lament for a vanishing America that echoed the same instincts behind "Okie from Muskogee" over a decade earlier. His personal life stayed turbulent throughout the period — multiple marriages and divorces, heavy drinking and drug use, and a notoriously undisciplined business life — even as the hits kept coming. In 1983 he teamed up with Willie Nelson for the album Pancho and Lefty, whose title track topped the charts and introduced him to a new generation of fans. By 1985 he'd been elected to the Nashville Songwriters Hall of Fame (1977) and was widely regarded, alongside Willie Nelson and a handful of others, as one of the last living links to classic honky-tonk country, even as Nashville's sound moved toward slicker, more pop-inflected production.

Merle Haggard: 1985-2015

Haggard kept recording and touring steadily through the late 1980s and 90s, though the sheer commercial dominance of his 70s and early-80s run gradually gave way to a more elder-statesman role in country music. He notched a late chart-topper with "Twinkle, Twinkle Lucky Star" (1987) and continued releasing albums even as radio increasingly favored younger artists, moving between labels including Curb and later Anti- and Vanguard. The 1990s and 2000s were defined less by hit singles and more by recognition: he was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame in 1994, and he kept working prolifically, including a well-received late-career album, 1996's 1996, and 2000's collaboration-heavy If I Could Only Fly, praised as one of his most personal records. In 2010 he received a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, and in 2011 he was one of the recipients of the Kennedy Center Honors, cementing his status as a foundational figure in American music rather than just a hitmaker. Health problems, including a lung cancer diagnosis and treatment in the early 2000s, shadowed his later years, but he continued touring almost until the end, often alongside his son Ben Haggard on guitar. He recorded a final duet album with Willie Nelson, Django and Jimmie, released in 2015. By the close of that year he was still performing, a living link back to the Bakersfield honky-tonks of the late 1950s.
